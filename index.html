Critical Care Billing & Documentation Reference

A working reference for intensivists at an academic center. CY2026 Medicare professional values (conversion factor $33.40); diagnosis CC/MCC status confirmed against the CMS MS-DRG v43 (FY2026) Appendix C list; DRG relative weights from the CMS FY2026 IPPS Final Rule Table 5.

Educational reference for documentation improvement — not coding, legal, or financial advice, and not an official CMS/AMA product. Verify against CMS before billing.

How payment works — two separate money streams

One ICU stay generates two independent bills that never come out of each other. Confusing them is the most common error.

Professional — that's you
Medicare Part B · Physician Fee Schedule · CPT / E&M codes
[(wRVU×wGPCI) + (PE×peGPCI) + (MP×mpGPCI)] × $33.40
Facility — the hospital (DRG)
Medicare Part A · IPPS · ICD-10 diagnoses & procedures
DRG relative weight × hospital base rate (~$6,752.61 national)

CPT / E&M codes generate your professional dollars (total RVU × conversion factor, adjusted by local GPCI; CY2026 non-APM CF $33.40, QP/APM CF $33.57). ICD-10 diagnosis codes add no professional RVUs — they establish medical necessity and drive the facility MS-DRG and its severity tier (CC/MCC). FY2026 national standardized operating base rate $6,752.61, before wage index/DSH/IME/outliers.

DRGs come in tiers — with MCC (highest) / with CC / without CC-MCC. The tier is set by the single highest-severity secondary diagnosis: one MCC maxes the tier, so the first MCC/CC captured is the valuable one. Capturing a CC is typically worth ~$1,200–$3,000; an MCC ~$3,000–$6,000+.

Common questions

Can a hospital get reimbursed for multiple DRGs in a single hospitalization?

Generally no — one MS-DRG per discharge. The entire stay groups to a single DRG based on the principal diagnosis, all secondary diagnoses (CC/MCC), procedures, and discharge disposition.

Outlier payments, New Technology Add-On Payments (NTAP), and DSH/IME adjustments can increase that one payment but are not additional DRGs. Genuinely separate admissions are separate DRGs; a same-day, same-hospital readmission for a related condition must be combined onto one claim (one DRG). A transferring hospital is usually paid a reduced per-diem rather than the full DRG.

Is the DRG billed separately from the physician component, or does the physician fee come out of the DRG?

Separate — the physician fee does not come out of the DRG. The DRG is the hospital's facility payment under Part A (IPPS). Your professional work is billed independently under Part B (Physician Fee Schedule, CPT codes).

They are two distinct claims with separate deductibles that don't net against each other. As an academic attending, if you're employed, your Part B payment may flow to the faculty practice plan — but it's still a separate claim, never part of the DRG.

What costs come out of the DRG?

The DRG is a bundled facility payment covering the hospital's cost of the stay: room/bed and ICU overhead, nursing, drugs and supplies, blood products, implanted devices, and the technical component of labs and imaging.

Not from the DRG: physician professional services (separate Part B). Think of it as everything the hospital itself provides, bundled into one payment per stay.

Once you've captured a single MCC, is there any point to capturing more CCs?

For this stay's Medicare MS-DRG payment: no extra dollars. One MCC already maxes the severity tier; additional CCs or MCCs don't push the same DRG higher.

But keep capturing them — it still matters:

  • APR-DRG / SOI-ROM (many commercial, Medicaid, and pediatric payers) do escalate with each additional secondary diagnosis.
  • Risk adjustment & quality: expected mortality, O:E ratios, and physician/hospital profiling depend on full comorbidity capture.
  • Audit resilience: if the one MCC is denied, recoded, or turns out to be the principal diagnosis, a second MCC/CC protects the tier.
  • Medical necessity & LOS: supports the documented level of care and length of stay.

Critical Care E/M — 99291 & 99292

Critical care is time-based, not complexity-based. Both must be true: (1) a critical illness/injury acutely impairing a vital organ system with high probability of imminent life-threatening deterioration, AND (2) high-complexity physician work directing care to treat/prevent it. ICU location alone, or a sick-but-stable patient, does not qualify.

Capturing time across multiple attendings (and APPs)

Document total critical care minutes in every note and let coding assign 99291/99292 — physicians should not self-select the codes. That one habit captures the most and prevents the common miss of billing 99291 but forgetting 99292.

  • Same group + same specialty aggregates. Two PCCM attendings in the same group are treated as one physician — their minutes on the same patient/same calendar day combine. The group reports 99291 once for the first 30–74 min, then 99292 for each additional 30. Example: 20 min + 85 min = 105 min → 99291 + 99292.
  • They cannot each bill 99291 (double-billing) — the time combines into one group claim. A different-specialty physician does bill a separate 99291.
  • Aggregation works even if neither attending reached 30 minutes alone — the combined time qualifies.
  • Each provider's time must independently be critical care, and simultaneous bedside time is counted once, not doubled.
  • With APPs in the group, same approach — both names & minutes in the notes; coding applies the FS modifier and split/shared logic (see Residents & APPs).
Ready-to-paste minute attestation — never a range, never the same number every patient “I personally provided ___ minutes of critical care to this patient on [date], exclusive of separately billable procedures and teaching time. The patient has [organ system(s)] at high risk of life-threatening deterioration; I reviewed [data], directed [interventions], and reassessed the response.”

What time counts (and what doesn't)

  • Counts: bedside time AND unit/floor time on that patient — reviewing labs/imaging, discussing with consultants/staff, documenting, and family discussions when the patient can't participate and the discussion drives management.
  • Doesn't: separately billable procedure time (carve it out), teaching time, time off the unit, time on other patients.

Billing with residents & APPs (academic settings)

Who may bill and whose time counts depends on whether you're with a resident (Teaching Physician rules) or an APP/NPP (Split/Shared rules). Don't conflate them.

Residents — Teaching Physician (TP) rules

  • Only the teaching physician's own time counts. Resident-alone time and teaching time don't count toward critical care minutes.
  • A resident can't bill critical care, and there's no split/shared with a resident. The service is billed under the TP.
  • The TP personally furnishes the billed critical care time and documents their own minutes + management. May refer to/combine the resident's note for history, exam, and assessment, but writes their own attestation and time statement.
  • Append modifier GC when a resident participated.
  • The primary-care exception / GE modifier does NOT apply to critical care.
Sample TP critical care attestation “I personally provided ___ minutes of critical care to this patient, exclusive of separately billable procedures and teaching time. I reviewed the labs/imaging/hemodynamics, directed vasopressor titration and ventilator changes, and I agree with the resident's documented history, exam, and assessment except as noted.”

APPs / NPPs — Split (or Shared) Visit rules

  • Applies when a physician AND an APP (NP/PA) in the same group both personally perform part of the same patient's care on the same calendar day in a facility setting. (Incident-to does not apply to hospital inpatient/ICU care.)
  • Critical care: sum the physician's and APP's minutes; whoever performed the substantive portion (>50% of combined time) bills. For time-based services, substantive portion = time only, not MDM.
  • Append modifier FS to any split/shared visit, regardless of who bills.
  • Document both individuals by name, each one's time, the combined total, and who did the substantive portion; the billing practitioner signs/dates.
  • Payment lever: APP-billed pays ~85% of the fee schedule; physician-billed (substantive portion) pays 100%.

Necessary elements of a billable procedure note

  • Indication / medical necessity — why now.
  • Informed consent (or documented emergency exception).
  • Time-out / patient ID for invasive procedures.
  • Technique & site — approach, laterality, anesthesia/prep, image guidance (and whether guidance is bundled).
  • Findings, specimens, devices placed (size, depth, confirmation — e.g., CXR for line position).
  • Complications (or explicit “none”) and post-procedure status.
  • Provider attestation + supervision/teaching-physician statement if a trainee assisted.
  • Time statement confirming procedure time was excluded from critical care minutes.

Procedures that drive the DRG — ICD-10-PCS

ICD-10-PCS is the facility procedure code set on the hospital claim — separate from the CPT codes you bill professionally. Every code is 7 characters, each an independent axis: section, body system, operation, body part, approach, device/duration, qualifier. The coder builds it only from your documentation and can't infer a missing axis — so what you write decides which code lands.

How to write the note so it’s captured

Ready-to-paste documentation lines “Invasive mechanical ventilation via ETT, continuous since [date/time]; remains ventilated.” (or “extubated [date/time]”)
“CRRT (CVVHDF), continuous, initiated [date/time], for acute kidney failure / ATN.”
“Percutaneous tracheostomy performed [date]; tracheostomy tube placed.”
“VV ECMO, peripheral cannulation, initiated [date/time]; ongoing.”
  • Timestamps over adjectives. Every vent/ECMO/CRRT duration code is computed from documented start/stop times.
  • Name the modality. Continuous CRRT vs intermittent HD; invasive vent vs BiPAP; VA vs VV ECMO.
  • Link the procedure to its diagnosis. CRRT only earns its keep when the acute kidney failure (N17, a CC) is documented and coded.
  • New procedure vs chronic status. A trach placed this stay drives 003/004; “chronically trached at baseline” is a status code and does not.

Educational documentation-improvement reference. PCS-to-DRG assignment changes annually; verify codes against the current ICD-10-PCS tables, the FY2026 MS-DRG Definitions Manual, and AHA Coding Clinic.

National, unadjusted (before wage index, DSH/IME, transfer/outlier). Ventilation/trach DRGs (003/004/207/208) are procedure-driven — vent hours >96 vs ≤96 (207 vs 208) is the single biggest lever.

Complex care & long length-of-stay

The chronically critically ill: capture severity, present-on-admission (POA) status, and ventilation duration. Some conditions forfeit DRG credit if hospital-acquired (HAC).

Pressure injury stage 3/4/unstageable = MCC (HAC if not POA). Vent >96h (PCS 5A1955Z) vs ≤96h (5A1945Z) is the biggest single lever. CLABSI/CAUTI = HAC if not POA.

Decompensated cirrhosis

Cirrhosis itself is only a CC; the decompensations win the severity. Code cirrhosis PLUS each decompensation present.

AKI in cirrhosis: hepatorenal syndrome (K76.7, MCC) vs ATN/prerenal (N17, CC) — the distinction changes the tier. Note: hepatic encephalopathy K76.82 is not a CC/MCC; capture hepatic failure with/without coma (K72.x) for severity.

Malnutrition

Severe = MCC; moderate/mild = CC. Requires physician documentation tied to ASPEN/GLIM criteria — an RDN note alone is not enough. BMI (Z68.x) does not establish malnutrition.

Note must contain: physician diagnosis + severity, etiology, criteria met (intake, weight loss %, muscle/fat loss, fluid, grip), intervention, and follow-up. Severe malnutrition (MCC) is often worth ~$3,000–$6,000+ on a stay with no other MCC.

Chronic trach-to-vent patients

Status codes don't drive the DRG. The procedure (ventilation hours) and acute respiratory failure move payment.

“Chronically vented at baseline” = chronic respiratory failure (CC). A new acute change (hypercapnia, pneumonia, failure to wean with decompensation) = acute-on-chronic respiratory failure (MCC) — document the acute change explicitly.
