Pulmonary Clinic Billing Reference

Pulmonary Clinic Billing & Documentation Reference

A working reference for the outpatient academic pulmonary clinic — where fellows and residents may be charting, APPs share the schedule, and the visit mix runs from a stable-asthma recheck to a PAH prostacyclin titration. CY2026 Medicare professional values (conversion factor $33.40).

Educational reference for documentation improvement — not coding, legal, or financial advice, and not an official CMS/AMA product. Outpatient and especially telehealth rules change frequently; verify current CMS, your MAC, and Ohio policy before billing.

How clinic payment works — no DRG, sometimes two claims

The ICU page's facility DRG does not exist in clinic — there is no Part A inpatient bundle. Outpatient is paid on the Physician Fee Schedule (PFS). The twist in an academic center: if your clinic is a hospital provider-based department, a second claim is generated — a hospital outpatient facility fee under OPPS.

Your professional dollars come from CPT / E&M codes (work + practice expense + malpractice RVUs × the conversion factor, adjusted by local GPCI). The same code pays a different amount depending on place of service: in a freestanding office you bill the higher non-facility rate (your practice expense is baked in); in a provider-based clinic you bill the lower facility rate because the hospital separately collects the facility fee. That single fact drives most of the academic-clinic billing nuances below.

Common questions

Why is my office E/M paid less when I move the same patient to the hospital-based clinic?

Because the practice-expense RVUs split out. In a freestanding office (POS 11) you bill the non-facility PFS rate, which bundles your overhead. In a provider-based/hospital outpatient clinic (POS 22 on-campus, 19 off-campus) you bill the lower facility PFS rate, and the hospital bills a separate OPPS facility fee (G0463) for the overhead.

The total dollars to the enterprise are often higher provider-based, but the physician line is lower, and the patient may face a separate facility-fee cost-share. This is a frequent source of confusion in academic departments.

Does the patient get billed twice for one provider-based visit?

Often, yes — two cost-shares. One for the professional service (your PFS claim) and one for the hospital outpatient facility fee. Patients are frequently surprised by the facility line; many institutions provide advance notice. It does not change your coding, but it is worth understanding when patients ask.

Can I bill a PFT (or an injection) on the same day as the office visit?

Usually yes, with modifier -25 on the E/M — if the E/M is a significant, separately identifiable service beyond the procedure's inherent pre/post work. The classic miss is performing spirometry and a biologic injection but forgetting the -25, so the payer bundles the visit. See PFTs, Infusions, and Modifiers.

Office vs provider-based clinic — the academic fork

Most teaching pulmonary clinics are provider-based departments of the hospital, not freestanding offices. That single classification changes the place-of-service code, your professional payment rate, whether a facility fee is generated, and — critically — whether incident-to billing is even available.

Office / outpatient E/M — 99202–99215

Since 2021, outpatient office E/M is leveled by medical decision making (MDM) or total time on the date of the encounter — whichever is more favorable. History and exam are performed as clinically appropriate but no longer drive the level. Pick the path that fits the visit; for a long counseling or care-coordination visit, time usually wins; for a quick but cognitively complex decision, MDM wins.

What counts toward total time

Total time = all the physician/QHP's time on the date of the encounter, face-to-face and not: reviewing the chart and outside records before the visit, the exam, counseling, ordering tests/meds, documenting in the EHR, and care coordination performed that day. It does not include time on a different calendar day, staff time, or time spent on a separately reported service (e.g., a PFT interpretation or an injection).

Time-based attestation — use a real number, never a range “Total time personally spent on the date of this encounter was ___ minutes, including pre-visit record review, the face-to-face visit, independent test interpretation, counseling and shared decision-making, ordering, care coordination, and documentation. This excludes any separately reported service.”

Prolonged outpatient time

Past the top of the highest level, prolonged-service add-ons apply — but Medicare and CPT diverge:

  • CPT / most commercial: 99417 — each additional 15 min, added to 99205/99215, starting once you pass the level's minimum time (75 min for 99205, 55 min for 99215).
  • Medicare: G2212 — same idea, but the clock starts later (after the maximum time of the level: 89 min for 99205, 69 min for 99215). Do not report 99417 to Medicare.

Confirm the current thresholds and your payer's choice; this pair is a common audit target.

Teaching physician rules & learners charting

In an academic clinic the chart may be touched by a fellow, a resident, or a medical/APP student before you sign. Each has different documentation rules. Pulmonary fellows are residents for Medicare teaching-physician (TP) purposes — they fall under TP rules, not split/shared.

Residents & fellows — Teaching Physician (TP) rules

  • The service is billed under the teaching physician. The TP must personally see the patient and personally perform (or be present for) the portion of the service that determines the level.
  • If leveling by MDM: the TP may verify the resident's documentation (history, exam, MDM) rather than re-document it, but must personally participate in management and indicate that in the note.
  • If leveling by time: only the teaching physician's time (alone, or with the resident together) counts. Resident-alone time does not count — a key contrast with APP split/shared, where both clinicians' time combines.
  • Append modifier GC when a resident/fellow participated.
Teaching physician clinic attestation “I personally saw and evaluated the patient with the fellow/resident. I reviewed the fellow's/resident's documented history, exam, and assessment, agree with them except as noted, and personally performed the medical decision-making for today's visit, including the plan documented below. (Time-based visits: only my personal time is counted toward the total.)”

Medical / PA / NP students

  • Since 2018, the TP (or the resident) may verify any student-documented component — ROS, PMH/social/family history, HPI, and exam — and need not re-document it.
  • But the TP must personally perform (or re-perform) the exam and the MDM and personally verify what the student wrote. Student documentation can support, not substitute for, the billable work.

The Primary Care Exception — usually NOT your clinic

At approved primary-care centers, the Primary Care Exception (PCE) lets the TP bill certain lower/mid-level visits (99202–99204, 99212–99214 and select preventive codes) without being present for the key portion, using modifier GE. A subspecialty pulmonary clinic generally does not qualify — assume standard TP presence rules apply and use GC, not GE, unless your GME/compliance office has specifically designated the site.

APPs — incident-to vs split/shared

For NPs and PAs, the right rule depends entirely on where the clinic sits. Getting this wrong is both a payment error and a compliance risk.

Freestanding office (POS 11) — incident-to is possible

  • An APP's service can bill under the physician's NPI at 100% if all incident-to conditions are met: the physician initiated care and set the plan, the patient is established with an established problem, the physician provides direct supervision (present in the office suite, immediately available), and the encounter is an integral part of the plan.
  • New patients and new problems cannot be incident-to. Those bill under the APP's own NPI at 85%.

Provider-based / hospital outpatient clinic — incident-to does NOT apply

  • In the facility setting, incident-to is unavailable. Either the APP bills under their own NPI (~85%), or — when a physician and APP in the same group both personally perform part of the same visit on the same day — split/shared rules apply.
  • Split/shared: the clinician who performs the substantive portion bills. For visits leveled by time, substantive = >50% of the combined time; for visits leveled by MDM, substantive = performed the MDM (confirm the current year's definition — CMS has shifted it). Append modifier FS.

Telehealth — Ohio-specific

Pulmonary is well-suited to telehealth for the right visits — PAP/CPAP adherence, stable ILD / PAH / sarcoidosis follow-up, post-discharge COPD checks, biologic-therapy management, tobacco cessation — while PFTs and exam-dependent encounters still need an in-person slot. Three layers of rules stack here: Medicare, the CPT code set, and Ohio (Medicaid + the state standard-of-care statute).

Volatility warning. Medicare's telehealth flexibilities have repeatedly approached a “cliff” and been extended at the last minute (most recently they lapsed 31 Jan 2026, then the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 — signed 3 Feb 2026 — restored and extended them). Confirm the current expiration date and your MAC's guidance before relying on any item here.

1) Medicare (as extended for 2026)

  • No geographic restriction; the patient's home is an eligible originating site for non-behavioral telehealth — extended through Dec 31, 2027.
  • Audio-only is permitted for non-behavioral telehealth through Dec 31, 2027 (when the patient can't or won't use video). Behavioral/mental-health visits keep their own in-person-waiver track (through Jan 1, 2028).
  • Coding for Medicare: report the usual office E/M (99202–99215) with POS 10 (telehealth, patient at home) or POS 02 (telehealth, location other than home), plus modifier 95 (synchronous audio-video) or 93 (audio-only).
  • Medicare does NOT accept the new 98000–98015 telemedicine codes — it kept the office-E/M-plus-modifier approach. It does recognize 98016 (brief virtual check-in).

2) The CPT telemedicine codes (CY2025+) — for non-Medicare payers

  • AMA added a dedicated series that replaced the deleted telephone codes 99441–99443: 98000–98007 synchronous audio-video, 98008–98015 synchronous audio-only, and 98016 brief synchronous check-in (5–10 min).
  • Some commercial and Medicaid plans want these codes; Medicare wants the office E/M + modifier 95/93 instead. Check each payer.

3) Ohio

  • Ohio Medicaid — OAC 5160-1-18 (in effect since 15 Jul 2022): telehealth means synchronous real-time audio + video (plus defined asynchronous activities). No geographic restriction — the patient can be anywhere, including home. Note ODM has not adopted modifier 93 for audio-only; follow ODM's current billing guidelines for how it wants audio-only and the 98000-series reported.
  • Ohio standard-of-care statute — ORC 4743.09 (from HB 122, effective 23 Mar 2022): the standard of care via telehealth must equal in-person care, and a provider–patient relationship may be established by telehealth (synchronous or asynchronous) if that standard is met. Implemented across the licensing boards (e.g., State Medical Board rules, OAC 4731-37-01).
  • Licensure follows the patient. You must be licensed in the state where the patient is physically located at the time of service — relevant for a referral region that crosses into KY/WV/IN/PA/MI. The Interstate Medical Licensure Compact can speed multistate licensure.

E-consults & virtual check-ins

Interprofessional consultation lets a pulmonologist weigh in on a case without a face-to-face visit — review the chart/imaging, render an opinion to the requesting clinician, and bill for the cognitive work. Requirements that make these payable

  • Patient consent documented (these carry cost-sharing) and the request documented in the record.

Pulmonary-fit E-consults shine for nodule triage and PFT interpretation, cutting wait times for a full visit; document consent, the question asked, your review, and your written recommendation.

E-visits — patient-portal / MyChart messages

Yes — billable. When a patient sends a message through the portal (MyChart) that needs real clinical work to answer, that asynchronous, patient-initiated exchange can be billed as an online digital E/M (“e-visit”). It is not for clerical or quick replies — it has to involve genuine evaluation and medical decision-making.

CPTCumulative time over 7 days~Medicare
994215–10 min~$15
9942211–20 min~$31
9942321+ min~$50

The guardrails that decide whether it's billable

  • Patient-initiated and an established patient — you can't bill a message you started, and these aren't for new patients.
  • Requires your medical decision-making. A refill done by protocol, a scheduling reply, or “your results look fine” with no decision doesn't qualify.
  • Counts cumulative time over a 7-day period (all your portal work on the issue, including review and the response), reported once per 7 days.
  • Bundling windows: not billable if it stems from an E/M in the prior 7 days (it's part of that visit's work); and if it leads to an office/telehealth visit within the next 7 days, the e-visit work rolls into that visit. Not billed on the same day as another E/M.
  • Consent to possible cost-sharing is documented (commonly a one-time portal consent).

Pulmonary-fit Example: an established asthma/COPD patient messages about worsening dyspnea and peak-flow readings; you review the trend, adjust the inhaler regimen, and document the decision — that's an e-visit, not a free message. Clinical staff who can't independently bill E/M use the non-physician equivalents 98970–98972.

Transitional care — the post-discharge window

After a hospitalization (COPD or asthma exacerbation, pneumonia, PE), Transitional Care Management pays well for owning the 30-day transition. It requires an interactive contact within 2 business days of discharge and a face-to-face visit within a window set by complexity.

Chronic & principal care management

Between visits, ongoing care coordination is billable monthly. CCM needs ≥2 chronic conditions; PCM is built for a single, complex, high-risk condition — which is exactly the subspecialty pulmonary situation (advanced COPD, severe persistent asthma, ILD, pulmonary hypertension).

When to bill — and yes, it's the between-visit work

This is the point most often missed: CCM/PCM pays for the non-face-to-face care you do between office visits — medication management, reviewing home data and outside results, phone calls, refills, prior auths, and coordinating with the PCP, pharmacy, or home-infusion company. It is not a visit code and does not require the patient to be in the room.

  • Billed once per calendar month, submitted at month-end once that month's time threshold is met — CCM 99490 ≥20 min clinical-staff time (or 99491 ≥30 min physician/QHP); PCM 99424 ≥30 min physician/QHP (or 99426 ≥30 min clinical staff). Time is cumulative across the month; add-on codes capture each additional increment.
  • No double-counting. Minutes already counted toward a billed E/M, TCM, the same month's other care-management code, or RPM/RTM management time can't also be counted here.
  • One practitioner per patient per month. A patient can't be billed CCM and PCM in the same month by the same practitioner — coordinate with primary care so you're not duplicating (often pulmonary bills PCM for the lung condition while PCP handles overall CCM, but only one care-management code pays per month).

Documentation needed to bill

  • Patient consent — obtained once (until they change practitioner), documented in the record, noting that cost-sharing applies, that only one practitioner bills per month, and that they may stop anytime.
  • A care plan in the EHR — comprehensive (CCM) or condition-specific (PCM), shared with the patient and revised over time.
  • A time log of the month's care-coordination activities (who, what, minutes).
  • The qualifying condition(s) — CCM: ≥2 chronic conditions expected to last ≥12 months (or until death) placing the patient at significant risk; PCM: one complex condition expected to last ≥3 months with frequent medication/regimen adjustment.
  • 24/7 access to the care team and continuity of care arrangement.
So is this billable between office visits, even in a month with no visit?

Yes — that's exactly what it's for. If your team accrues the monthly time threshold managing the qualifying condition(s) outside of a visit, you bill CCM/PCM for that month whether or not the patient was seen. In a month that also has an office visit, you can bill both — just don't count the visit's time (or any other separately billed time) toward the care-management minutes.

Infusions, injections & biologics

The severe-asthma biologics and PAH therapies make drug administration a real revenue and documentation area. Two separate things are billed: the administration code (the act) and the drug itself (a HCPCS J-code, usually buy-and-bill).

Administration codes carry tiny work RVUs — the payment is almost entirely practice expense, so the dollar amount swings sharply between office (non-facility) and provider-based (facility, where the hospital bills the facility fee). Bill the drug separately with its HCPCS J-code and document drug, dose, and units administered, plus wastage: append JW for the discarded amount of a single-dose vial, or JZ to attest no wastage. Pulmonary-fit Most asthma biologics are subcutaneous (96372); reslizumab and IVIG are IV infusions. Provider-based status materially changes infusion economics (facility fee + drug handling).

If a significant, separately identifiable E/M (e.g., the assessment deciding to continue/adjust the biologic) is done the same day, append -25 to the E/M. J-codes update frequently — verify the current HCPCS before billing.
